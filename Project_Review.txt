RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND THEIR EVIDENCE
RQ1. How did total arrivals, overnight/same-day composition and source composition
change between 2019 and 2025?
Daily and annual counts, source shares and signed source changes answer this.
Arrivals increased from 39,406,181 to 40,069,360 (+1.68%), while the overnight share
fell from 47.28% to 41.29% (about 5.99 percentage points). Mainland arrivals rose by
1,093,945, more than the total net increase of 663,179 because other groups offset
part of that rise. The 164.95% contribution to net change is not a visitor share or
a causal effect. Source groups follow the official travel-document definition;
overnight visitors are not equivalent to hotel guests or room nights.

RQ2. How did the typical level, spread and upper quantiles of daily arrivals change,
and did changes vary by month and weekday?
The mean rose from 107,962 to 109,779 visits per day, but the median fell from 104,695
to 103,252. IQR increased from 27,163 to 34,032 and P90 from 138,130 to 148,850.2.
ECDFs, shared-bin histograms and month/weekday comparisons show why recovery of the
annual total does not imply recovery of a typical day or an unchanged distribution.
These are historical descriptions, not tests of statistical significance.

RQ3. How did calendar models selected using 2024 validation perform in 2025, and how
did trend and Ridge affect errors?
The design starts with 2023 training data, uses four expanding training windows
with the quarters of 2024 for validation, and refits on 2023-2024 before evaluating
2025. Preprocessing is fitted inside each training fold. Model and alpha selection
use the unweighted mean of the four validation MSEs. Validation RMSE is the square
root of that mean, not the mean of four RMSEs.

Ridge with alpha=100 wins validation (RMSE 24,820.82 visits/day). On the 2025 holdout,
its RMSE is 25,818.77, MAE is 18,767.15 and R-squared is 0.2143. OLS with trend has a
lower holdout RMSE of 23,916.19 and R-squared of 0.3258, despite much poorer average
validation performance. Reporting that reversal is necessary; it does not justify
switching the selected model after observing the test results. The large first-fold
OLS-with-trend error is numerically reproducible, not a transcription error; its
cause has not been established by a formal collinearity or dependence analysis.

Using the training-period P90 of 121,707, 103 test dates exceed the threshold.
Ridge RMSE is 36,510.33 on those dates versus 20,115.40 on the other 262 dates.
Observed-minus-predicted bias on the high-arrival dates is +27,053.09 visits/day,
showing substantial underestimation. The grouping uses observed test arrivals as a
post hoc diagnostic; it is not a classifier that identifies future busy dates.

RQ4. Did arrivals, nominal non-gaming expenditure and official spending per visit
recover together under same-quarter and annual comparisons?
Quarterly arrival sums are joined one-to-one with 16 published spending estimates.
Compared with 2019, 2025 annual arrivals rose 1.68%, published nominal expenditure
rose 25.04% and official annual spending per visit rose 23.00%. Compared with 2024,
2025 expenditure rose 6.32% but official spending per visit fell 7.28%. These findings
are compatible because the comparison years differ. For 2025 Q3, overnight visitors
accounted for 41.68% of arrivals and 82.22% of non-gaming expenditure, with official
spending per visit of MOP 3,846 versus MOP 595 for same-day visitors. This describes
composition and spending intensity; it does not quantify a causal composition effect.

DEPTH AND LIMITS
The daily series is substantial for this course: nine years of complete daily data
support multiple comparisons and a separate validation/test period. The 55,879 source
rows add composition detail, not 55,879 independent visitors. The 16 expenditure
quarters are a small, complementary aggregate dataset, so they support descriptive
comparisons rather than a complex spending model.

The substantive depth comes from:
- tracing data quality and checking sensitivity rather than silently cleaning flags;
- comparing totals, typical days, spread and upper quantiles together;
- separating source contributions, visitor-type shares and spending per visit;
- comparing a simple baseline, OLS, trend and regularisation under fixed time splits;
- examining validation/test disagreement and errors on high-arrival dates;
- reconciling daily, quarterly and annual units and using explicit comparison bases.

The evidence supports uneven recovery and limited calendar-based predictability.
It does not explain why individuals visit or spend, identify policy effects, establish
statistical significance or demonstrate operational forecasting readiness. The 2025
outcomes were excluded from fitting and selection but had been viewed in EDA, so the
whole study is not fully blind. Adjacent days may be dependent; residual autocorrelation
was not formally tested. Calendar inputs omit moving holidays, weather, events and
other drivers. Spending is nominal MOP, excludes gaming and comes from published quota-
survey estimates without individual records, survey weights or sampling variances.
No adjustment for inflation or formal decomposition of visitor composition is made.

No numerical correction or new analytical method was needed in this English revision.
Translation preserves these boundaries, including the distinction between the model
selected by validation and the model with the lowest observed holdout error. Team
members still need to understand and explain the work and complete the deferred
submission materials before treating the package as ready to submit.
